# Product-mechanics map

Use this reference to identify the dominant return engine and risk transmission of a product. Adapt it to the exact legal terms and holdings; the table is a starting map, not a substitute for the prospectus.

| Product | Where return ultimately comes from | Dominant repricing variables | Material loss routes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bank deposit / certificate of deposit | Bank pays contracted interest from its lending and funding business | Contract rate, reinvestment rate, inflation | Bank failure beyond applicable protection, early-withdrawal penalty, inflation eroding purchasing power, reinvesting later at lower rates |
| Money-market fund | Interest on very short-dated government, bank, and corporate instruments | Policy rates, short-term funding spreads | Credit/liquidity stress, rare NAV impairment, falling income after rate cuts; it is not automatically a guaranteed deposit |
| Government bond | Coupons and principal promised by the sovereign; market-price changes when required yields move | Policy path, inflation expectations, term premium | Rising yields reduce price, especially for long duration; inflation damages real return; currency and sovereign risk may dominate for foreign bonds |
| Corporate bond / bond fund | Coupon and principal plus spread compression or roll-down | Risk-free yield, credit spread, default/recovery expectations | Default or downgrade, spread widening, rate rise, forced selling and liquidity stress; a bond fund normally has no fixed repayment date for the investor |
| Equity / broad equity ETF | Dividends and long-run growth in company earnings/cash flows, plus changes in valuation multiples | Earnings, margins, discount rates, valuation, risk appetite | Recession, competition, dilution, governance failure, rate-driven valuation compression, concentration and permanent business loss |
| Nasdaq-100 ETF | Cash flows and valuation of large non-financial Nasdaq-listed constituents, often with heavy technology exposure | Growth expectations, long-term yields, large-cap tech earnings, valuation and positioning | Sector/factor concentration, high starting valuation, rate shock, regulation, earnings disappointment; ETF tracking, fees, FX and premium/discount add wrapper risk |
| Foreign/QDII fund | Underlying foreign asset return translated through the fund | Underlying market plus FX, quota, local and home-market liquidity | Currency loss, cross-market trading-hour gaps, quota-driven premium, tax/fee drag, tracking difference, settlement and policy changes |
| REIT | Rent and property operating cash flow distributed to investors, plus property valuation changes | Occupancy, rent growth, capitalization rates, financing costs | Vacancy, tenant stress, leverage/refinancing, rate increases, property concentration, asset devaluation and liquidity shocks |
| Gold / commodity exposure | Price appreciation; futures-based products may also earn or lose collateral yield and roll return | Real yields, USD, scarcity, inventories, geopolitics, positioning | No operating cash flow, long flat periods, currency reversal, storage/roll cost, futures curve losses, tracking and leverage |
| Savings insurance / annuity | Insurer's contractual and/or participating payouts backed by its asset portfolio | Guaranteed rate, declared participation, insurer investment return | Long lock-up, surrender loss, non-guaranteed illustrations, inflation, insurer credit/operational risk, complex exclusions and currency mismatch |
| Bank wealth-management product | Income and repricing of the underlying bond, credit, equity, or derivative portfolio | Depends on holdings and valuation policy | It is generally not a deposit guarantee; credit, NAV fluctuation, liquidity gates, maturity mismatch, fees and opaque look-through exposure |
| Structured deposit / structured note | Deposit/bond component plus option payoff linked to an index, rate, FX, commodity, or stock | Barrier/strike, volatility, path, maturity, underlying level | Conditional payoff, cap on gains, path/barrier event, early-exit loss, issuer credit; “principal protection” may apply only at maturity and under exact terms |
| Leveraged or inverse ETF | Daily derivative exposure multiplied or inverted, minus financing and fees | Daily underlying return, volatility, path, rebalance cost | Compounding decay and path dependence over multiple days, gap loss, leverage, derivative counterparty and tracking risk; not simply a long-horizon multiple |

## Explain “why higher expected return” without promising it

A return premium is normally payment for bearing one or more scarce and unpleasant risks:

- **Equity premium:** company profits are residual; shareholders are paid after employees, suppliers, lenders, and taxes, so cash flows and valuations are uncertain.
- **Term premium:** long-maturity lenders lock money up and bear greater inflation and rate uncertainty.
- **Credit premium:** private issuers can default and become illiquid during stress.
- **Liquidity premium:** the asset may be hard or costly to sell exactly when cash is needed.
- **Complexity/insurance premium:** option sellers or structured-product buyers transfer asymmetric risks, but fees and issuer margins can absorb much of the apparent yield.
- **Currency premium is not automatic:** FX exposure can diversify or harm; it does not reliably pay a positive premium to every investor.

The premium is expected over many possible futures, not owed in each year. A high purchase valuation can turn strong business growth into weak investor return because the starting price already embeds optimistic expectations.

## Compare “safe” and “risky” on several axes

Do not compress risk into one score. Compare:

1. contractual certainty of cash flow;
2. probability and severity of permanent loss;
3. ordinary price fluctuation and maximum historical drawdown;
4. time needed to recover;
5. liquidity and exit cost;
6. inflation and currency match to the user's spending;
7. issuer/counterparty dependence;
8. complexity, leverage, and conditions hidden in the payoff;
9. concentration and interaction with the user's existing holdings.

A low-volatility nominal product can still be unsafe for a long-horizon real-spending goal if inflation consistently exceeds its return. Conversely, an equity ETF can be unsuitable for next year's tuition even if its long-run expected return is attractive.

## Wrapper checklist

After explaining the underlying asset, inspect the wrapper for expense ratio and other fees, tracking difference, bid-ask spread, premium/discount, assets under management, market-making/liquidity, securities lending, collateral and counterparties, leverage/reset frequency, hedging, tax, distribution policy, redemption restrictions, maturity, guarantee wording, and jurisdiction. Discuss only material items, but do not omit a feature that changes the payoff.
